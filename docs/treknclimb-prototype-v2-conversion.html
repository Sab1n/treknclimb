Altitude sickness: what our guides actually watch for

14 Jul 2026 · updated 3 Sep · 7 min · Bishnu Gurung, 22 seasons

Every guide we employ has turned a group around at some point. It's the least popular decision in the job and the only one that matters.

The three-symptom rule

Acute mountain sickness starts as a headache paracetamol won't fully clear, plus any one of nausea, dizziness, breathlessness at rest, or broken sleep. One symptom alone is common and usually passes. Two or more that persist through a rest day is our threshold for descent.

We ask about sleep every morning because poor sleep is the earliest reliable signal — and the one people dismiss as jet lag or a bad mattress.

Our itineraries build in the extra day

Every Nepal trek we run has two acclimatisation days, not one. Send your dates and see the day-by-day plan for yourself.

Get a free itinerary

Why our itineraries look slower

A twelve-day Everest Base Camp itinerary exists and plenty of companies sell it. It works for roughly seven in ten trekkers. Our fourteen-day version adds acclimatisation days at Namche and Dingboche, which moves that closer to nine in ten.

The descent rule

Descent is the only reliable treatment. Oxygen and medication buy time to descend — they aren't a substitute for it. If a guide calls it, the group doesn't vote.
